If I go off of the frame measurements alone (58cm seat tube), this bike should be way too tall for me at    5 '7". But it's not. Part of it, surely, is the swap from drops to flats, but I would still have thought it would be too long. Granted, it’s pretty much impossible to stand over the top tube without shoes on, but the idea was to build it, sell it, then get my hands on a smaller frame.

This build is my most recent, from a Univega Gran Rally frame which came with a full Shimano 600 groupset of which I saved only the cranks, front sprockets, and brakes. It was in great shape with some lovely drilled brake levers which I have been trying to find for ages. Of course, I could have bought them for outrageous prices on ebay but it’s much better to buy bikes for parts than buy parts for bikes.

### Bike building for fun and profit

**Step 1:** Find a cheap road bike frame. In this case, a late 80’s Univega gran rally. Make sure it has semi horizontal or horizontal dropouts so that you can tension your chain. Try to get one with as many parts as you can reuse as possible, such as a nice wheelset and good brakes. Try and get one with a freehub instead of a freewheel but this might push you out of the cheap range.

**Step 2:** Swap out the drops for flats. Or bars of your choosing. While the bars which came on the bike might work fine, bars are the single most important part of the bike when it comes to your enjoyment while riding. Flats also look great. I found mine for ten bucks on craigslist. 

**Step 3:** Get a single speed freewheel after looking up gear ratios and deciding on the best one for your needs (or single speed conversion kit if you have a freehub, if you don’t know which one you have look up “sheldon brown cassette vs freewheel”). I got a Strumey Archer one for $20. I don’t recommend it. If you’re going to splurge on any part, make it this one. The issue is that you need one which is designed for 3/32” size chains. This is because your front chainrings are sized for 3/32” and we don’t want to have to replace them. Unfortunately this limitation will rule out a lot of the nice cheap freewheels meant for ½” bmx chains. Your options will be limited. If you have a freehub then you can buy any old single speed conversion kit. The quality of this is much less important.

**Step 3 (alternate):** Don’t touch the rear freewheel/cassette. Find a nice gear and line up the chain with whichever of the front chainrings create the best chainline (the straightest chain). This will save some money but won’t look as cool. Never underestimate how much aesthetics count towards the full experience. However, if your build is rusty and looks like something out of mad max then reusing the old freewheel might actually be a positive in the aesthetics column. 

**Step 4:** Shorten the chain if it’s not too rusted, or get a new one if it is. Don’t spend more than 20 bucks on this. Use a chain tool to shorten the chain, making sure that the wheel fully engages with the rear dropouts. Make sure the chain is tensioned, pulling back on the wheel so that when you tighten it down the chain is as tight as you can make it. IMPORTANT: Make sure the chainline is not too angled. An angled chain will be less efficient and wear out much faster. I usually pick the inner front chainring (the one closest to the bike) as it creates an acceptable chainline most of the time. If you got a single speed conversion kit then you can easily use any combination of the included spacers to get the best chainline with either of the front chainrings, depending on the gear ratio you want.

**Step 5:** Attach brakes, grips, and cabling. You may have to replace any/all of these parts. This all comes back to getting a frame with as many reusable parts as possible. 

Step 6: Profit.

### Riding 

The first time I took off on the bike to rip around the block I popped the chain off and nearly crashed into a parked car. The second time I learned why all those fixed gear people were so cultish about it. There are no thoughts in your head except for spinning your legs as fast as you can. It’s the most meditative experience I’ve ever had on a bike.

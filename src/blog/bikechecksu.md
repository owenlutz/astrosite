If I go off of the frame measurements alone (58cm seat tube), this bike should be way too tall for me at    5 '7". But it's not. Part of it, surely, is the swap from drops to flats, but I would still have thought it would be too long. Granted, it’s pretty much impossible to stand over the top tube without shoes on, but the idea was to build it, sell it, then get my hands on a smaller frame.

This build is my most recent, from a Univega Gran Rally frame which came with a full Shimano 600 groupset of which I saved only the cranks, front sprockets, and brakes. It was in great shape with some lovely drilled brake levers which I have been trying to find for ages. Of course, I could have bought them for outrageous prices on ebay but it’s much better to buy bikes for parts than buy parts for bikes. This is also the third Unviega I’ve acquired this year. 

### Building 

I bought the frame off a nice guy on craigslist for $85, which was a steal as I could have ridden it away from the sale with no issues. Some derusting of the components and a new chain and this bike would have been a great ride. However, I had bought the bike mainly for the groupset and wheels which I wanted to put on a smaller frame, and after I had stripped the frame the parts all went on the shelf and the frame got hung up above my dresser. 

Fast forward two months and I desperately wanted a fixed or single speed. My road bike just wasn't doing it after the move back to the city. I was more concerned with trying to not get run over than being in the right gear and it seemed like I’d finally have to move away from my beloved downtube shifters. There is also a certain aesthetic quality to a single speed, one that just makes you ride faster even if technically you’re being less efficient. Obviously those New York messengers were up to something when they all started riding track bikes in the 80’s. The road bike just didn’t inspire me to ride out through traffic like the single speed did. And so the somewhat obsessive monitoring of the local used market began. 

The frame sitting on the wall didn’t even register as a potential base for the build for weeks, until I realized I had never built up a single speed before and it might be a good idea to have a practice run which I could just sell when I was finished with the bonus of having a little extra cheddar for the real build. And crucially, it had semi-horizontal dropouts for chain tensioning. 

The bars, Titec flats of about 570mm width, I got off craigslist for 10 bucks. The karv grips, Shimano claris levers, and cabling from another craigslist find for 25. And a new chain for 15 with a single speed freewheel from Sturmey archer for 20. So, for $155 I had a single speed and some parts to boot. Not bad. I may have shortened the chain just one link short, so the rear axle has a rather tenuous grip on the dropouts, but it’s not fixed so hopefully it’ll be fine as long as I keep an eye on it. Oh and the Strumey archer freewheel kind of sucks, making this weird clicking noise as I pedal along.

The issue of having vintage wheels using a freewheel design as opposed to a freehub is that I have no ability to adjust the chainline by moving the rear freewheel without re-dishing the rear wheel (moving the hub requires basically a full wheel rebuild) which I was not nearly patient enough to do. If I had a freehub I could have just bought a single speed conversion kit and used spacers to dial in the chain line. 

This led to the use of the inner sprocket on the front so that the chain wouldn't be too crazily angled. It’s still not perfect, but it’s way better. I also kept the outside sprocket on the front because that stepdown would have looked strange. That and some completely seized chainring bolts. Oh well. I just told everyone it’s there on purpose as a bashguard.

### Riding 

The first time I took off on the bike to rip around the block I popped the chain off and nearly crashed into a parked car. The second time I learned why all those fixed gear people were so cultish about it. There are no thoughts in your head except for spinning your legs as fast as you can. It’s the most meditative experience I’ve ever had on a bike. I really should have sprung for a better freewheel though as that clicking is starting to get on my nerves. 

Despite the size of the frame it’s also supremely comfortable. I love a rock solid saddle and my favorite ones by far are those old vettas that used to come stock on 80’s road bikes so I had no problems with the no name saddle that was on the bike when I bought it (one day I’ll buy a brooks …). The chunky silicone grips do a great job at minimizing vibration which makes up for the thin and cracked tires. I have a new set but I figured I’d burn through these first. The seat is about as low as it can go. It’s so nice that I had qualms about listing it, and I’m dreading the day that someone actually shows up when they said they would.
